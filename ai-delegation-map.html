AI Delegation Map

Which decisions may AI make, recommend, or never touch?

Every organisation adopting AI agents is drawing this map, whether it means to or not. We help leaders draw it deliberately, with the controls each category needs.

The delegation map

Three categories of decision authority

Every decision handed to AI passes through the same five steps and lands in one of three zones.

  1. DecisionName the decision and its owner.
  2. Risk classificationRate consequence, reversibility and regulatory effect.
  3. Delegation authorityPlace it in category A, B or C.
  4. MonitoringLog, sample and watch for drift.
  5. AccountabilityA named person answers for the outcome.
A

Category A · Agent-executed

Within explicitly authorised boundaries

AI agents execute predefined activities inside hard limits on value, volume and data. People monitor and can stop them.

Enterprise examples
  • Invoice matching under a value cap
  • Routine service requests
  • Data quality checks and reconciliations
  • Scheduling and first drafts
B

Category B · Human approval required

Agent analyses and recommends; a person authorises

AI prepares the analysis or the action. A named person approves, amends or rejects it, with reasons recorded.

Enterprise examples
  • Credit memos and claims above a threshold
  • Supplier changes and purchase orders
  • Pricing proposals
  • Customer remediation offers
C

Category C · Human-reserved

Decisions remain under direct human authority

AI may inform, never act. Reserved for decisions with high consequence, low reversibility, legal effect on individuals or a need for legitimacy.

Enterprise examples
  • Lending decisions about individuals
  • Clinical judgement
  • Hiring, dismissal and discipline
  • Safety-critical and emergency operations

Category placement supports governance decisions. It does not by itself determine legal or regulatory compliance.

Controls and accountability

What each category needs

The more autonomy an agent has, the stronger the controls around it, not the weaker.

Decision ownershipA named process owner; the agent has its own identity and an accountable ownerA named approver for each decision type, recorded with the decisionA named decision-maker; AI outputs are inputs only
Approval thresholdsHard limits on value, volume, counterparties and data the agent may act onThresholds above which a person must approve, with dual approval for the highest tierNot applicable: every decision needs a person

Monitoring and audit trailsEvery action logged with inputs, tools used and outcome; sampled reviewRecommendation, approval and rationale logged; acceptance rates monitored for rubber-stampingAI inputs retained where used, so the basis of the decision can be reconstructed
Escalation rulesAutomatic escalation on exceptions, low confidence, limit breaches or unusual patternsEscalation to a senior approver when the recommendation is outside policyEscalation follows existing delegated authority

Model and vendor oversightTesting before deployment and after changes; vendor model changes notified and re-testedValidation of the model and its data; performance tracked by segmentVendor tools assessed for data handling, security and suitability before use
A living map

How decisions move between categories

  • Decisions move outward, towards more autonomy, only when evidence shows the agent performs within limits over a defined period.
  • Decisions move inward when incidents, drift or regulatory change require it. Moving a decision inward should never need a project.
  • The map is reviewed by its owner on a fixed cycle and reported to the board with the Agent Value Ledger.

Try it

Where might a decision sit?

Think of one decision you are considering handing to AI, and answer five questions. The result is a starting point for discussion, not a determination.

If this decision were wrong, could it be reversed quickly and cheaply?
Could a single wrong decision cause material financial loss or harm?
Does the decision have legal or similarly significant effects on an individual?
Is the decision regulated, or does it require a licensed or accountable person?
Is there a track record showing the AI performs reliably on this task?

Answer all five questions to see a suggested category.

Singapore AI governance guidance

How the map aligns with published guidance

The map reflects themes common to Singapore's published guidance, including risk-based human involvement, accountability and life-cycle controls.

  • Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI ↗22 January 2026
    IMDA (developed); announced by the Ministry of Digital Development and Information

    Guidance on deploying AI agents responsibly: assess and bound risks upfront, keep humans meaningfully accountable, apply technical controls across the agent lifecycle, and enable end-user responsibility.

    Announcement ↗
  • Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence Risk Management (financial institutions) ↗Consultation 13 November 2025; response to feedback published 7 October 2026
    Monetary Authority of Singapore

    Expectations for board and senior management oversight, AI inventories and risk materiality assessment, life-cycle controls and third-party AI. Published summaries indicate phased application from October 2027.

  • Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI ↗30 May 2024
    IMDA and the AI Verify Foundation

    Nine dimensions for a trusted generative AI ecosystem, including accountability, data, trusted development and deployment, incident reporting, and testing and assurance.

  • Model AI Governance Framework, second edition ↗21 January 2020
    Personal Data Protection Commission (PDPC) and IMDA

    The foundation of Singapore's approach: internal governance, determining the level of human involvement in AI-augmented decisions, operations management, and stakeholder communication.

  • AI Verify testing framework and toolkit ↗International pilot May 2022
    IMDA; now under the AI Verify Foundation

    A testing framework and software toolkit for demonstrating responsible AI through standardised tests and process checks.

References are provided to show how the AI Delegation Map aligns with published guidance. Kiros Intelligence Group is not certified, accredited or endorsed by any of these bodies, and using our frameworks does not by itself demonstrate compliance.
